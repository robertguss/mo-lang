## 1. Verdict

**Narrow.** Continue Mo as a small, explicitly falsifiable research instrument about the
agent feedback loop, while stopping or wrapping the lines that the project's own evidence
has already damaged: first-party TLS, the BEAM-superiority framing (already suspended,
correctly), and product-scale ambitions (registry, full MCP surface, mored-as-specced).
The next unit of work should not be a feature or a service; it should be the ablation the
project has never run: **the same agent workflow on an equivalent scaffold bolted onto an
existing language**, because the packet's own premise admits the language-layer null
hypothesis stood after rounds 1–6, and nothing since has retested it.

- **Strongest reason for this verdict:** the one thesis component that has survived every
  measurement is the feedback loop (0.4–0.8 s to a verdict vs. 7–14 s for baselines;
  first-fix rates above 0.9 per diagnostic across four models; 12/12 regenerations of
  program bodies from the spec altitude). Whether that loop *requires* Mo's semantics or
  is mostly tooling that could be retrofitted is precisely testable and decision-changing,
  and Mo — uniquely — already has both the instrument and the corpus to test it.
- **Strongest objection:** the binding constraint on "reliable software from affordable
  models" looks model-shaped, not language-shaped. The worst observed failure (Haiku
  missing the scheduling requirement) occurred in all three languages; the contract-catch
  ledger is 0-for-6 generations; and the measurement instruments themselves have returned
  false greens. If the ablation shows parity, Mo's language and dual runtime are overhead,
  and the right product is agent tooling for an existing language. That outcome is live,
  not hypothetical.
- **Confidence:** moderate, qualitative. I am most confident about the TLS and instrument
  recommendations, least confident about how much of the loop advantage is
  semantics-bound.

## 2. Understanding

**Goal, restated:** make it cheap and trustworthy for AI agents — including affordable,
open-weight models — to build and *maintain* reliable networked software, with humans
supplying requirements and judging behavioral evidence rather than reviewing source.
**Proposed means:** a new statically typed, capability-passing, BEAM-inspired language and
runtime whose compiler, tests, seeded simulator, contracts, and inspection surface form
one machine-consumable loop, with a first-party/generated supply chain. **Assumptions:**
agents are the users (G1); a corpus-less language can be taught just-in-time (G4);
restrictions prevent more cost than they impose (G5); first-party code reduces net risk
(G6). **Verified knowledge is much thinner than the ambition:** a working toolchain and
six real programs exist; the loop-speed and frontier-model-reliability results are real
but small-sample and partly self-measured; the contract-catch claim, small-model floor,
TLS, durability, and the runtime-superiority claim are respectively unproven, unproven,
damaged, open, and suspended.

**Access disclosure.** I worked in a checkout at exactly the anchor commit `986fdc7`. I
read `HANDOFF.md`, `mo-wiki/state-of-the-project.md`,
`mo-wiki/spec/design-v0/01-premise.md`, `mo-wiki/plans/roadmap.md` (top),
`audit/README.md`, and `examples/recipes/model-client.mo`, and listed `toolchain/src/`
and `examples/`. I did **not** run the toolchain, tests, or benchmarks, did not read the
Zig/C sources beyond confirming the named files exist, did not open evidence bundles or
seek sealed suites, and did not poll the audit channel or act as lead. So I could
independently check that the packet's inventory and historical numbers **match the
repository's own records** (loop times, round 9 counts, the 27-limbo/63-64 TLS counts,
the gen-6 zero-catch ledger, the instrument false-green probes, the fsync finding, the
suspension language in premise/roadmap/audit README) — but not that those records are
true. Externally, I verified all three cited sources and they say what the packet says:
the compiler-feedback paper compares one baseline attempt to five feedback attempts and
measures compilation with similarity proxies; LSP `failureHandling` makes workspace-edit
atomicity a client capability (`undo` explicitly has "no guarantee"); `--runxfail` runs
xfail-marked tests "as if it weren't marked at all." One contradiction to report:
`state-of-the-project.md` concedes chapter 2 "still lists [counted limits] as laws" while
they were reclassified as settings — a documented, not silent, inconsistency; older
thesis prose survives below explicit "historical" markers.

## 4. Alternatives and ranked actions

Comparing credible alternatives on equivalent obligations (reliable agent-maintained
services, low total cost, supply-chain control, verifiable evidence):

1. **Existing-language agent toolkit (Elixir/OTP + Mo-style harness):** JSON diagnostics,
   error catalog, seeded property tests, contract macros, capability *discipline* (not
   enforcement), independent acceptance runner. Gets perhaps 70% of the loop for a
   fraction of the cost; loses static capability enforcement, compiler-enforced bounds,
   and deterministic simulation (genuinely hard on BEAM); keeps mature TLS/OTP and
   distribution. This is the alternative Mo must beat, and Robert already lives there.
2. **Typed frontend over a mature runtime (Gleam v1, March 2024):** static types,
   exhaustiveness, no-null/no-exceptions on the BEAM with the ecosystem intact. Closest
   existing thing to "Mo's checks on Erlang's runtime"; lacks capabilities,
   deadlines-by-construction, and Mo's toolchain integration. A serious comparator arm,
   cheaper than maintaining Mo.
3. **Current custom stack (Mo):** unique bundle (capabilities + enforced bounds + sim +
   integrated loop) at the cost of dual-runtime maintenance, a corpus-less language, and
   sole-maintainer supply chain.
4. **DSL/library only:** weakest — the things worth having are exactly the ones libraries
   can't enforce.

**Ranked actions.** *Keep:* the diagnostics/first-fix measurement culture, the corpus +
hidden-suite discipline, the crypto-brick differential pattern, deadlines/bounded
mailboxes as semantics, the audit-role separation. *Change:* wrap TLS (platform or
vendored maintained implementation behind the capability API) and re-scope "zero
dependencies" to "zero unvetted application packages"; repair instruments with negative
controls before trusting any count; finish reclassifying counted limits as settings; make
mailbox overflow a consumable backpressure result pending E5. *Defer:* registry/security
lifecycle, full MCP surface, time-travel/hot-reload/projectional editing, syntax
revisions, user-parameterized generics unless a representative app blocks. *Drop:*
completing first-party TLS to production grade — the largest avoidable investment; step
39's E/F plus perpetual maintenance buys a worse artifact than wrapping, and the
27-limbo checkpoint is the predicted outcome, not bad luck. *Constraint I accept:*
Robert's taste for a Ruby/Elixir-shaped language — syntax familiarity is cheap to keep
and the bodies-as-cache result says body syntax matters less than the spec layer.
*Preference I challenge:* "first-party everything" as identity rather than as a
per-component cost-benefit.

## 5. Research/experiment proposals (max five, ranked by information per cost)

**E1 — Language vs. scaffold ablation (the decision-changer).** *Hypothesis:* Mo's
semantics reduce attempts/cost to independently verified behavior beyond what its tooling
style provides. *Competing explanation:* the loop's value is tooling, retrofittable
anywhere. *Setup:* 6–10 pre-registered maintenance changes on the existing
job-queue/notes apps; three arms — Mo; Elixir with a "Mo-flavored" harness (JSON check
output, error catalog, seeded tests, independent runner); plain Elixir. Same models (one
frontier, one affordable), fresh sessions, N≥5 per cell. *Independent oracle:* hidden
behavioral suites authored under the audit workflow, run by a harness outside the agent's
writable tree. *Metrics:* verified-outcome rate, attempts, tokens/cost, wall time,
escalations. *Confounders:* Mo unfamiliarity (report Mo-with-primer separately),
harness-quality asymmetry (have the auditor review arm parity), task selection. *Cost:*
mostly model spend plus harness port; no new language work. *Actions:* positive → invest
in the semantics-bound bundle and design Program 7's replacement around it; negative →
wind the language down, export the tooling; inconclusive → rerun with more tasks before
any new build.

**E2 — Contract value by seeded defect injection (rescues R-B from underpower).**
*Hypothesis:* `never`/`invariant`/contracts catch injected realistic defects that each
program's own tests miss. *Competing explanation:* contracts only mirror test coverage.
*Setup:* inject 30–50 mutation-style defects (drawn from the erosion rounds' real defect
taxonomy) into corpus programs, run with contracts on/off. *Oracle:* ground truth is the
injected defect; hidden suites arbitrate "would tests have caught it." *Metrics:*
incremental catch rate, false-positive rate, runtime overhead (gen-4's 9× is the
cautionary tale). *Confounders:* injected defects unrepresentative — mitigate by sampling
from observed defects. *Cost:* small; toolchain already has `mutation.zig`. *Actions:*
positive → the catch claim earns its row ahead of generation ten; negative → per R-B,
contracts stay as tools and leave the claim; inconclusive → the natural-catch ledger
continues but stops being load-bearing.

**E3 — Onboarding ablation with a fine-tune arm.** *Hypothesis:* a short versioned primer
+ error-directed lookup beats cold discovery; *and* for small models, a validated-corpus
fine-tune beats both. *Competing explanation:* frontier models don't need the primer;
small models can't use it (the literature's pattern). *Setup:* fresh-session app tasks
under four arms (cold / primer / primer+lookup / small-model fine-tune trained on a
MultiPL-T-style corpus auto-generated and validated by Mo's own toolchain — a missing
opportunity the packet's proposal omits). *Oracle:* protected behavioral checks.
*Metrics:* round trips, verified success, cost. *Confounders:* serving variance
(dedicated inference), task memorization across sessions. *Cost:* moderate (one fine-tune
run). *Actions:* positive → ship the primer, start the corpus lane; negative for small
models → G2 narrows to "affordable frontier-tier models," stated honestly.

**E4 — Diagnostic-explanation ablation.** *Hypothesis:* Mo's explain-why/rule/next-step
diagnostics reduce repair loops vs. terse errors, holding everything else fixed.
*Competing explanation:* any compile-fail signal drives the fix (the arXiv study shows
iteration fixes *compilation*; it says nothing about explanation quality or behavior).
*Setup:* toolchain flag emitting terse variants; same tasks/models both ways.
*Oracle/metrics:* first-fix rate per diagnostic (already instrumented), loops to verified
behavior. *Cost:* small. *Actions:* positive → the "Elm for machines" bet earns evidence
worth publishing; negative → stop polishing prose, spend on lookup latency.

**E5 — Mailbox overflow policy under burst load.** *Hypothesis:* must-consume
backpressure `Result` yields higher service availability and agent repair success than
crash-the-sender. *Setup:* burst-load maintenance task on the queue under both policies.
*Oracle:* hidden availability/loss checks. *Cost:* small runtime change behind a flag.
*Actions:* decides one contested language rule with data instead of taste.

## 6. Program 7 and the first trial

**Program 7 (Q7): replace, smaller, later.** Suspension was the right call and is
honestly recorded (no gate declared passed; sealed materials preserved). `mored` as
specced — RESP + persistence + ACLs + TLS + metrics under hidden suites — was
overreaching: it stacked an adversarial protocol (TLS, now known-broken), an open
durability boundary (macOS full-sync, directory-sync), and instruments that demonstrably
return false greens. The replacement should be a **maintenance-under-fault comparative**
on the existing queue/kv programs: N pre-registered changes, auditor-authored hidden
behavioral suites, Mo vs. Elixir (optionally the Gleam arm), measuring verified-outcome
rate, recovery behavior, and total cost — i.e., E1 grown into the versioned experiment.
Falsifiable claim: "on matched maintenance tasks, agents reach independently verified
behavior in Mo at ≥ the Elixir arm's rate and ≤ 1.5× its cost" — with
negative/inconclusive interpretations precommitted before the seal. Prerequisites, in
order: instrument repair with negative controls; TLS wrap decision; durability scoped to
Linux until Darwin full-sync lands. Preserved obligations: T-A/T-B/T-C and R-B remain
live and should re-host onto this workload; the retired S-A/S-B/S-C rows should be
reported historically as "not passed, superseded," and the replacement must not quietly
re-litigate them. What remains unanswered from the old framing: whether
capabilities/recipes hold under a real service (T-rows) and whether the laws catch
anything (R-B) — both survive into E1/E2.

**First trial (Q8): run it, but call it what it is — an apparatus shakedown.** The 401
policy change (`examples/recipes/model-client.mo`; its intent line currently mandates
retry-on-error, so this is a spec change, correctly not labeled a defect) is a small,
localized edit any competent model can make in any language. It can establish that the
full loop, attempt accounting, and independent verdict machinery work end-to-end under
interpreted and compiled execution. It cannot establish anything about Mo's value,
small-model floors, or the runtime thesis. Anti-gaming: the recipe's tests are
agent-visible, so acceptance must rest on auditor-held hidden checks against the scripted
endpoint's request log — 401 → exactly one request then the status error; 500/timeout
paths still retry; deadline semantics preserved; a skip-all-requests or disable-retries
hack fails those cases by construction; tests/sidecar edits are detected by running
acceptance from a tree the agent cannot write. Cheapest stronger alternative: run the
same policy change simultaneously as one task inside E1's three arms, so even the
shakedown yields a calibration point. Transfer is validated later by the replacement
Program 7, not by this trial.

**Most important unanswered question for Robert:** *if the ablation shows an equivalent
scaffold on Elixir matches Mo, do you want to keep building the language anyway?*
Answering this before E1 runs is what separates a research program from a hobby with
instruments — either answer is legitimate, but the experiment's value depends on being
willing to act on it. Other open uncertainties (non-blocking): the unverified
private-worktree restoration; whether `.mo.ids` identity should grow rename-stability;
where read-authority ends and secret exposure begins on the inspection surface.
