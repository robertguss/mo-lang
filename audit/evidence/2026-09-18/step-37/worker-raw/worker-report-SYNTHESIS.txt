Part A (f8d8dfc): the brick, in toolchain/src/bricks/tls.zig
  The brick gains the client half of TLS 1.3, as an engine over bytes on the same record layer
  as the server. mo_tls_connect makes a client's connection and mo_tls_conn_new a server's;
  every export after that is the same for both.

  The client:
  - sends a ClientHello with X25519, the two suites, SNI for a name, Ed25519 and ECDSA P-256
    SHA-256, and ALPN when offered, and answers one HelloRetryRequest;
  - checks the transcript, the CertificateVerify against the leaf's key, and the server's
    Finished, then sends its own Finished;
  - carries records both ways, answers and starts KeyUpdate, sends and reads close_notify, and
    reads session tickets and drops them.

  The chain check:
  - at most five certificates, each signed by the next, up to a root in the trust set;
  - every issuer a CA;
  - the leaf for the host (DNS and IP SANs, the CN only when there are no SANs);
  - every certificate's dates at now_sec;
  - refusals send the alert OpenSSL 3.0 sends for the same chain.

  Other changes:
  - ALPN on both sides, with no_application_protocol when both lists share nothing.
  - New exports: mo_tls_client_new, client_offer, server_offer, connect, protocol, key_update,
    client_free, untrusted.

  Tests:
  - Zig's own tls.Client over both suites and both key types, four sessions each for the
    megabyte and the small handshake;
  - the swallowed TestTimedOut now reported by name;
  - the RFC 8448 section 3 trace replayed through the client, its ClientHello, Finished,
    application record and close_notify equal to the trace byte for byte;
  - brick to brick: each suite, each key, with and without ALPN, a megabyte both ways;
  - chains of three and five accepted, and each refusal produced from a chain built for it;
  - ALPN's cases, KeyUpdate from each side with records after, one HelloRetryRequest, and the
    five rejections fed to the client.

  Fixtures: examples/effects/tls/gen.sh (openssl ca, dated 2025-01-01 for ten years) and the
  34 PEM files it wrote.

======================================================================
NOTE: A CONN CANNOT BE READ AND WRITTEN AT ONCE
======================================================================
A Conn does not read while a write on it is waiting (net.zig's `writing` flag). So a Mo
program that writes lines while the runtime's `lines` loop reads the same connection stalls:
- the write waits for the peer;
- the peer waits for its own write, which waits for us to read;
- our read waits for our write.
It clears only at the write's deadline: 1,600 lines of 4 KiB took 58.5 s, whether the writes
ran from main or from a process. A Mo program cannot stream both ways through one Conn at once
today; this matters for program 7.

Separately, neither runtime sets TCP_NODELAY. A Mo client that writes a window of 4 KiB lines
and then reads it back waits out the peer's delayed ACK, about 40 ms a window: 3,200 lines took
8.98 s in windows of 16, and 0.39 s in windows of 256.

tls-dial.mo works around both by keeping two windows of 64 lines in flight. Neither runtime
behaviour was changed in this step.

DECISIONS THE BRIEF DID NOT COVER
======================================================================
1. The fixtures and gen.sh went into part A, not part C, because the brick's chain tests need
   them.
2. I added refusal fixtures beyond the brief, each for both key types: a leaf with its
   intermediate left out, a six-certificate chain, a five-certificate chain (accepted), a leaf
   signed by a non-CA, and an RSA leaf.
3. Refusal alerts follow what OpenSSL 3.0 sends for the same chain.
   - A chain past five certificates, an untrusted root, a missing link, or a non-CA issuer is
     unknown_ca, not bad_certificate.
   - A wrong name, or a date not yet begun, is bad_certificate.
   - A bad certificate signature is bad_certificate (RFC 8446), where OpenSSL sends
     decrypt_error.
4. Every issuer between the leaf and the root must be a CA (basicConstraints), and the root's
   own dates are checked too.
5. The chain is checked strictly as sent, each certificate signed by the next. The brick does
   not reorder or skip extra certificates, which RFC 8446 says a client should tolerate.
   OpenSSL's s_server must be given the chain without the leaf repeated.
6. Zig's Certificate.parse and der.Element.parse index DER with no bounds checks, so a hostile
   certificate could panic them. The brick checks each certificate's shape before parsing it:
   certificates off the wire, the trust PEM, and the server's own leaf.
7. Zig's Parsed.verify is replaced by the brick's own check (Ed25519, and ECDSA P-256 with
   SHA-256). Its RSA code took the brick's compile time from 8.6 s to 14.4 s; it is now 10.3 s.
   P-384 alone added 2.5 s, so P-384 and SHA-384 chains are refused as unsupported_certificate.
   Real-world chains through P-384 CAs are therefore refused.
8. Zig 0.16's own client cannot check an Ed25519 CertificateVerify. The two Ed25519 cells of
   the Zig-client matrix assert its TlsBadSignatureScheme answer. Full Ed25519 sessions are
   covered against OpenSSL and the brick's own client.
9. A test-only suite preference (Server.prefer, default AES-128-GCM) makes Zig's client land
   on ChaCha20.
10. The RFC 8448 replay uses test-only hooks: the trace's random, X25519 key and empty session
    id; the trace's hello layout (suites, and extensions around the key share); and the chain
    check skipped, since the trace's certificate is RSA. It compares the ClientHello, Finished,
    application record and close_notify byte for byte, and reads the ticket and the server's
    data.
11. The offer exports return a new object, so a TlsClient or TlsServer is a value in the rows.
    A name that is empty, over 255 bytes, or contains a NUL is a crash.
12. A plaintext alert is accepted during the handshake.
13. The client does what common clients do:
    - sends a 32-byte random session id (middlebox-compatibility mode, with the one
      change_cipher_spec that goes with it);
    - sends no SNI for an IP address, and checks IP SANs instead;
    - checks a NewSessionTicket's shape and drops it;
    - answers a requested KeyUpdate at once.
14. A new export, mo_tls_untrusted, is how both runtimes map a refused chain to Untrusted.
15. Under the fixture, connect and accept run the peer's engine and deliver messages to other
    processes, as Http.send's fixture does. So a test runs the server in a process that serve
    feeds, and dials in one statement.
16. The fixture's lines loop on a TLS connection scanned ciphertext, a step 36 bug never
    exercised. It now reads through the engine, in both runtimes.
17. The C runtime printed a TlsServer as "a Runtime". It now prints "a TlsServer", "a Tls" and
    "a TlsClient", as the interpreter does.
18. tls-client.mo's asserts also accept the Timeout or Closed a seeded fault run makes, because
    the corpus runs process tests under 100 seeds with faults. A scratch copy without that
    escape passes all 6 tests under both runtimes, so the plain run is strict.
19. tls-client.mo exits 1 on failure (# exit: 1), and prints the error's name.
20. The differential run drives the brick's side through a Zig peer over its exports, not a Mo
    program. No Mo row starts a KeyUpdate, and the suite and alert aren't visible to a program.
21. s_server -stateless drops the connection after the brick's change_cipher_spec, so the
    client's cookie HelloRetryRequest is not tested against OpenSSL. It is tested only brick to
    brick. The differential run's retry is s_client's -groups P-256:X25519.
22. The echo data uses an alphabet with no s_client or s_server command letters and no
    character that s_server prints. s_server's echo is read only after its connection report.
23. The fuzz driver is a test executable built by zig build tls-tools, so the brick's test
    hooks exist. The brick gains three test-only openings: the TestHooks.sent capture, a public
    handshakeBytes, and public fixtures. The driver has a 2 s watchdog per step, and fuzz.py
    checks itself with a planted panic and a planted hang. The budget is CPU time from
    RUSAGE_CHILDREN.
24. OpenSSL 3.0's s_time -new exits 1 on a good run, even against OpenSSL's own s_server. So
    handshake.py's exit-code check accepts 0 or 1 with the count printed and no error line.
25. handshake.py --pairs step36 serves step 36's own certificates from git, so its numbers
    compare like for like. With the new chain, rates drop a fifth to a third, from the client's
    extra verification.
26. A SIGKILL to guard.py orphaned the echo under it. stop() now sends SIGTERM first, in both
    benches.
27. Step 36's unstamped outputs were removed from work/. The originals are in
    audit/evidence/2026-09-17/step-36/worker-raw/.
28. measure.py makes the self-signed leaf at run time with openssl req -x509. The "before" mo
    was built from 6f449c9 in a worktree, which was removed afterwards. A rate is
    count / (t(count) - t(0)).
29. measure.py's client keeps two windows of 64 lines in flight, because of the Nagle and
    read-while-write behaviour in the note above.
30. The part A and part B suites ran in worktrees holding exactly each part's files, so every
    commit is the tree its suite tested. The two timed-out runs are reported above.
